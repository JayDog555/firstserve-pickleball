You do not need much for a first open play session. Comfortable shoes, a paddle (yours or a borrowed one), and water cover most of it. This checklist works in any US city. The venue's own page and the sign at the court always come first.

## Check the venue's page before you pack

Open play rules are set by each park, recreation center, or club, so read the official page for the place you plan to visit before you go. Look for:

- **Open play days and times**, and whether they change by season.
- **Fees**, such as a drop-in fee, a guest fee, a membership, or a parking fee.
- **Reservations or registration.** Some indoor drop-in sessions ask you to sign up in advance or sign a waiver.
- **Skill labels**, such as beginner, social, 2.5, or intermediate courts.
- **Whether loaner paddles or balls are provided.** If the page does not say, call the number listed.

Our guide to [finding beginner-friendly open play](/guides/find-open-play/) explains how to find your city or county parks page and how to read a reservation page. Our [US city list](/cities/) links to the city pages we have verified so far.

## The short checklist

1. Court shoes or tennis shoes
2. A paddle, or a plan to borrow one
3. A couple of balls that match the setting (indoor or outdoor)
4. A full water bottle
5. Layers you can add or remove
6. Sunscreen, a hat or visor, and sunglasses for outdoor courts
7. A small towel
8. Your phone, a way to pay any posted fee, and your ID or membership card if the venue asks for one

## Shoes

Wear court shoes or tennis shoes with lateral support. Pickleball has a lot of short side-to-side steps, and road-running shoes are built for forward motion. If you are playing indoors, check whether the gym asks for non-marking soles. Many indoor facilities do.

## A paddle, or a loaner

If you already own a paddle, bring it. If you do not, you have a few options:

- Ask the recreation desk or the venue whether it lends paddles. Some community centers and beginner clinics do. The venue's page or a quick phone call will tell you.
- Ask the friend who invited you whether they have a spare.
- Ask politely at the court. Regular players sometimes carry a second paddle, but do not count on it.

If you decide to buy before your first session, a modest starter paddle is enough. Our [first paddle guide](/guides/first-paddle/) covers budget options, buying used, and which specs matter.

## Balls: indoor vs. outdoor

Outdoor balls are harder and have smaller holes. Indoor balls are softer and have larger holes. Using the wrong one makes the ball bounce and fly differently, so match the ball to where you are playing. Many open play groups supply balls or share them, but bringing two or three of the right type is a small courtesy.

## Water and a small towel

Bring a full water bottle, especially at outdoor courts. Not every park has a working drinking fountain near the courts. A small towel helps with sweat on your hands and grip, and gives you something to sit on if benches are hot or wet.

## Layers

Mornings and evenings can be cooler than midday, and indoor gyms vary. Wear a light layer you can take off between games. Athletic clothes that let you move side to side are enough. You do not need anything sport-specific.

## Sun protection for outdoor courts

Most public park courts have little or no shade. Bring sunscreen, a hat or visor, and sunglasses. Reapply sunscreen during a long session.

## Optional, but useful

- **Eye protection**, especially at busy courts where balls come over from the next game.
- **A small snack** if you plan to stay for more than an hour or two.
- **A light bag** to keep your paddle, water, and keys off the court.
- **A paddle cover** if you are carrying a paddle in a bag with keys or other hard items.

## When you arrive

Watch a game or two, find out how the paddle queue works, and tell the next group you are new. The [open play guide](/guides/find-open-play/) explains paddle-stacking etiquette and skill levels in plain language.
